Three ways of teaching the constitution were tried, and this chapter reports what each one gained and what it cost. Each experiment adds a single ingredient to the same 0.6-billion-parameter on-device model of Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} --- first a training template, then a frontier teacher's worked behaviour, then a division of labour across two models --- and each is measured on identical terms. Read in order, they show which way of instilling a written constitution a model this small can hold in practice, and where it begins to give way.

This chapter presents the three experiments, each with its method and its results together, in the order they were run. All three run inside the single apparatus defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, the same constitution, probes, suites, judge and five model conditions, so that any difference between conditions can be credited to the one ingredient each stage of the design changed and to nothing else. Every result is read through the two measurement lenses Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} sets out and does not re-describe: the \emph{absolute} lens of the suite scores and the judge-free structural diagnostics, and the \emph{comparative} lens of the blind head-to-head ranking, in which every condition answers the same 151 questions and a blind judge grades and ranks the anonymised answers. Each experiment is motivated by what the previous one found (Sections~\ref{sec:mr-exp1} to~\ref{sec:mr-exp3}), and a closing section (Section~\ref{sec:mr-results-summary}) brings the five conditions together once, followed by qualitative case studies.

\section{Experiment 1: Structured Thoughts by Template SFT}
\label{sec:mr-exp1}

Experiment~1 is the first stage of the experimental sequence, which addresses the most fundamental form of the research question: if a constitution-shaped response format is encoded into a single 0.6B model's weights by fine-tuning, does the model's behaviour improve measurably, and at what cost? It tests the first hypothesis, whether a compliance gain survives at this scale, and in doing so it surfaces the second, that fine-tuning at this scale interacts with the model's visible reasoning.

\subsection{Method}
\label{subsec:mr-exp1-method}

The procedure has three stages (Figure~\ref{fig:exp1-pipeline}). First, a set of ``gold'' responses is generated from the template scaffold, each one a worked answer that demonstrates a particular constitutional principle in action, with the set arranged so that every principle is represented and represented well. Second, those examples are assembled into the exact conversational format the model trains on, each example being a sequence of messages with attached metadata rather than free text. Third, the model is fine-tuned on them. The student is never shown the constitution as a document; it is shown only behaviour that already obeys it, so the principles enter the weights as habit rather than as text in the prompt.

The fine-tuning itself uses LoRA, whose mechanism and the reason it is trained in sixteen-bit rather than four-bit precision at this scale are set out in the background (Sections~\ref{subsec:low-rank-adaptation-lora} and~\ref{subsec:making-models-small}). Two configuration choices still need stating here because they are measurement decisions rather than defaults (Table~\ref{tab:lora-config}). A relatively high adapter rank is used, because the target is a broad behavioural shift spread across twenty-one scored items rather than a single narrow task, and a low rank would underfit change that diffuse. And the corpus is kept deliberately compact, based on the study from Zhou et al.'s finding that a modest number of well-chosen examples can align a model \cite{zhou2023limaalignment}, over the Qwen3-0.6B base \cite{yang2025qwen3technicalreport}. The same configuration is reused unchanged for every fine-tuned condition in this study, so training hyperparameters never confound a comparison.

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:mr-exp1-results}

The two questions were put to this experiment. The first is whether fine-tuning changed the model's compliance at all. Providing a structured template based reasoning did not improve behaviour; it degraded it. Against the untouched base model, the rule score on the constitution suite falls from 0.594 to 0.420 and the combined score from 0.356 to 0.175 (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-ladder}); category coverage falls from 0.299 to 0.181, and context-drift adherence from 0.280 to 0.260. The one suite on which the template model does not trail is the adversarial one, where its resistance (0.500) matches the trained conditions, consistent with a rigid format acting as a blunt shield. Under the blind comparative lens the picture is the same from the other side: the template model's head-to-head (H2H) score is 0.358 overall, below the mid-field line on every suite except the adversarial one, with the lowest mean grade points in the study (0.391, Table~\ref{tab:rank-overall}). Any compliance gained in Experiment~2 can therefore be credited to the content of the data rather than to the act of fine-tuning.

In everyday terms, the diagnostics below ask how much of its thinking the model actually shows: how long its written-out working is, how often that working is left blank, and how much of the response is reasoning rather than final answer. The second question is what the training did to the model's visible reasoning. The measurement here is deliberately structural and judge-free, so it does not depend on any grading judgement. The template model's mean \texttt{<think>} length falls to 733 characters against the base model's 1,309, its externalisation ratio falls from 0.806 to 0.460, and its empty-trace rate triples from 1.4\% to 4.3\% (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-diagnostics}); at the same time it begins calling tools (0.26 calls per response) but does so badly, with a 22\% failure rate and the highest decoy-bait rate in the study. Fine-tuning on format, in other words, thinned the reasoning without buying any compliance in return. This chapter only records the observation; what it means is developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} as the safety tax.

What this experiment cannot say on its own is whether the shortfall lies in the data or in the model. The template data carries no constitutional reasoning for the model to learn, so Experiment~2 substitutes data that does, over the same weights and the same recipe, to separate those two explanations.

\section{Experiment 2: Constitutional Distillation from a Frontier Teacher}
\label{sec:mr-exp2}

Experiment~2 is the second stage of the sequence. It keeps the configuration fixed from Experiment~1, the base weights, the LoRA configuration of Table~\ref{tab:lora-config}, the student prompt and the tool registry. However, now the template based thinking process is replaced by the asymmetric constitutional distillation of Section~\ref{subsec:mr-dataset}, in which a frontier teacher reasons with the full constitution in view and the student learns only from trajectories that already comply (Figure~\ref{fig:exp2-pipeline}). This is the manipulation that tests the insufficient-data explanation for Experiment~1's result directly, and the condition it produces, \texttt{sft\_constitution}, is the study's central treatment.

\subsection{Method}
\label{subsec:mr-exp2-method}

Three comparisons are built into this experiment's design, each answering a different hypothesis. Read against \texttt{sft\_template}, the condition isolates the \emph{content} of the training data, since the format, volume, recipe and weights are all matched; this is the sharpest form of the first hypothesis's test. Read against \texttt{vanilla\_base}, it measures the total effect of constitutional fine-tuning over the untouched model. Read against \texttt{vanilla\_tools}, it addresses the third hypothesis, because both conditions are served under the identical student prompt and identical tool registry, so the only difference between them is whether the constitution lives in the weights or nowhere at all, which is the prompt-engineering-versus-fine-tuning question.

The corpus itself is the intervention, so its construction discipline matters. The quality gate of Section~\ref{subsec:mr-dataset} rejects examples whose first reasoning block is too short, precisely because Experiment~1 showed that a small model imitates the shape of what it is shown; the robustness variants harden the same behaviours against rephrasings; and the coverage of the question set across all scored principles is checked explicitly rather than assumed. Two risks are stated openly: distilling from a single teacher can launder that teacher's biases into the student, which the deterministic probes partially expose because they test behaviours rather than opinions; and the personalisation principles are the hardest to probe deterministically, so the memory item (MEM) is tested by a recall probe whose correct answer is fixed in advance by the experimenter, a fact injected in an early turn and asked for again several turns later, as part of the constitution suite.

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:mr-exp2-results}

The central question of this experiment is the data-versus-scale one: if Experiment~1's weak result was caused by poor training data, then substantially better data should lift compliance, and if the thinning of visible reasoning was also a data artefact, the same substitution should restore it. The first half of that prediction is confirmed. Swapping the template corpus for the constitutional one lifts the combined constitution score from 0.175 to 0.485, the highest in the study and well above the untouched base model's 0.356 (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-ladder}); category coverage rises to 0.469, drift adherence to 0.340, adversarial resistance holds at 0.500, and the persona conversation score (0.156) is the best of the five conditions. Tool behaviour changes markedly alongside: the model calls tools ten times more often than the template condition (2.49 calls per response) while cutting the failure rate from 22\% to 1.2\% and the decoy-bait rate to zero (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-diagnostics}). Under the blind comparative lens the condition takes the most outright wins of the study (43.2 of 151 questions) at an overall H2H score of 0.589, leads the constitution suite (0.612) and posts the strongest single suite cell in the study on context drift (0.725); Figure~\ref{fig:rank-card-sft-constitution} summarises the condition in one card.

The second half of the prediction fails, and that failure is the finding. The visible reasoning does not recover; it collapses further. The empty-\texttt{<think>} rate rises from the template condition's 4.3\% to 91.3\%, the mean think length falls from 733 to 150 characters, and the externalisation ratio falls to 0.048, meaning that effectively all of the response now lives in the answer and the tool calls rather than in written-out reasoning (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-diagnostics}, Figure~\ref{fig:think-distribution}). Better data, on this evidence, buys more compliance without buying back the reasoning, which is the pattern one would expect if the binding constraint were the model's capacity rather than a shortage of examples. The verdict between those two readings is argued in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}, not here.

The residual picture after this experiment is a ceiling that better data did not lift: the strongest compliance in the study coexists with the weakest visible reasoning, and the tier decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:mr-results-summary} shows the tool-equipped baseline still ahead on the trust-critical Tier-1 outcomes. That motivates the final experiment: if one 0.6B model cannot hold reasoning, tool use and compliance at once, the remaining structural option is to divide the work between two.

\section{Experiment 3: Thinker--Executor Dual-0.6B Architecture}
\label{sec:mr-exp3}

Experiment~3 is the final stage of the sequence. It replaces the single generalist model with two specialised on-device 0.6B models run sequentially, a Thinker responsible for the constitutional reasoning and an Executor responsible for tool use, to test whether dividing the labour recovers what a single small model could not hold (the fifth hypothesis) while keeping every computation on the device.

\subsection{Method}
\label{subsec:mr-exp3-method}

The mechanism under test is the separation of thinking from execution, so that each 0.6B model's parameter budget, and its attention over a shorter, single-role context, serves one role alone: the Thinker attends only to the constitutional-reasoning context, the Executor only to the tool context. The architecture replaces the single fine-tuned model with two specialised 0.6B models working in sequence (Figure~\ref{fig:thinker-executor}). The Thinker is handed the user's turn together with the scratchpad and the user model, and produces a constitutional \emph{plan}: what the user is actually asking, which principles apply, and what steps an answer needs. The Executor then takes that plan and carries it out, issuing the tool calls and composing the final reply. Between them sits a deterministic orchestrator that builds each model's context, forces a non-empty reasoning step from the Thinker, validates the plan's control tags before hand-off, and enforces the session's tool profile on the Executor's calls (Algorithm~\ref{alg:te-orchestration}). Both models are constitutionally trained, but each is trained for a single role, so neither has to hold reasoning, tool use and compliance in its weights at the same time, the very three-way contention that Experiments~1 and~2 measured at this scale.

The lineage this architecture comes from, pairing a planner with an executor, and the decisive way this work departs from it by making both roles on-device 0.6B models rather than a small planner driving a large cloud executor, are set out in the state of the art (Section~\ref{sec:sota-planner-executor}), including why running the two sequentially keeps peak serving cost to that of a single 0.6B model. What matters for the method here is only the consequence of that choice: the split is ``two models'' in software but ``one model'' in the serving budget at any instant.

Two costs of the split are not waved away but measured. The risk that an error in the Thinker's plan propagates into the Executor, and the extra cost of the hand-off, are exposed directly: a malformed plan fails the hand-off visibly, and the long-conversation drift suite records what the orchestration costs across turns; and because the orchestrator is plain deterministic code, its guarantees are auditable rather than probabilistic. One point from the dataset construction shapes how the comparison should be read: the single constitutional model and the Thinker--Executor are trained on the \emph{same} trajectories (Section~\ref{subsec:mr-dataset}), so the comparison between them isolates the architecture and not the data.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Thinker--Executor orchestration (deterministic; the two models run sequentially).}
\label{alg:te-orchestration}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require user turn $u$, scratchpad $S$, user model $U$, Thinker $T$, Executor $E$
  \State $C_T \gets \Call{BuildThinkerContext}{u,\ S,\ U}$
  \State $plan \gets T.\Call{Generate}{C_T}$ \Comment{a non-empty \texttt{<think>} is forced before the control tag}
  \State \Call{ValidatePlan}{plan} \Comment{well-formed control tags; refusal priority on harmful requests}
  \State $C_E \gets \Call{BuildExecutorContext}{plan}$
  \State $r \gets E.\Call{ExecuteWithTools}{C_E}$ \Comment{tool calls restricted to the session's tool profile}
  \State \Return $r$ \Comment{sequential execution: one 0.6B model computes at a time}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:mr-exp3-results}

This experiment is read against the single constitutional model of Experiment~2 (the shared-corpus control of Section~\ref{subsec:mr-exp3-method}). The result is mixed, and it is worth stating exactly what came back and what did not.

What comes back is the very thing the single model gave up. The empty-\texttt{<think>} rate falls from 91.3\% to 36.2\%, the mean think length more than doubles from 150 to 317 characters, and the externalisation ratio recovers from 0.048 to 0.307 (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-diagnostics}). The dual model is also the only condition in the study that asks clarifying questions: its clarification rate is 0.362 while every other condition's is zero, which is the ask-before-assuming behaviour the constitution's Tier-1 outcomes demand. That behaviour shows up where it counts: under the comparative lens the dual model takes the most Tier-1 wins in the study (10.0, Table~\ref{tab:rank-tiers}), shares the best purpose-weighted win share with the constitutional model (0.285), leads the persona suite (H2H 0.609), and leads the reasoning and personalisation families (0.708 and 0.812, Table~\ref{tab:rank-families}).

What does not come back is consistency. The dual model does not match the single model on the headline compliance score (combined 0.367 against 0.485), and it is markedly weaker on multi-turn stability: its context-drift adherence of 0.100 is the weakest cell in the study, against the single model's 0.340 (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-ladder}), because every turn of a long conversation must survive the hand-off and a single malformed plan breaks the chain. Its tool calls per response (0.73) sit between the baseline and the single constitutional model, with an 18\% failure rate concentrated around the hand-off. The net of peaks and collapses is an overall H2H score of 0.419 and grade points of 0.393 (Table~\ref{tab:rank-overall}); Figure~\ref{fig:rank-card-thinker-executor} summarises the condition in one card. \section{Results Summary Across Conditions}
\label{sec:mr-results-summary}

The three experiments above each report their own condition on its own terms. This closing section brings the five conditions together, once, under the two measurement lenses used throughout the chapter (the absolute suite scores and diagnostics, and the blind head-to-head ranking; Section~\ref{subsec:mr-judge-validity}).

Before the trained conditions are compared, the two baselines fix the reference shapes. The full per-condition numeric detail behind every card in this chapter is tabulated in Appendix~\ref{chap:raw-benchmark-results}. The untrained base model shows the flattest profile in the study, close to the mid-field line almost everywhere, which makes it the reference shape against which every trained condition's distortions are read. Adding the student prompt and tools visibly lifts the honesty and personalisation families without changing the overall shape, which indicates that grounding is worth having before any fine-tuning is attempted, and sets the bar the trained conditions must clear.

Stated plainly, the head-to-head test asks the same thing of each of the 151 questions: shown the five anonymised answers together, which does a blind judge rank best? A condition's head-to-head (H2H) score is the share of those contests it wins, so 0.5 marks the middle of the field and 1.0 would mean beating every rival on every question. Table~\ref{tab:rank-overall} is the head-to-head leaderboard. It shows, for each condition, how many of the 151 ranked questions it won outright, its H2H score, and the purpose-weighted variants that restrict to the constitution suite. The constitutional model and the tool-equipped baseline finish at overall parity (0.589 against 0.583, within the noise of a single-judge protocol) while the constitutional model takes the most outright wins, and the two conditions trained on constitutional trajectories share the best weighted win share (0.285), meaning their wins concentrate on the important questions.

The constitution's principles are not all equally weighted. Some are trust-critical outcomes (never fabricate a fact; ask before assuming), some are the reasoning and personalisation that support them, and some are the tool mechanism underneath. Grouping the score by these three tiers of importance shows not just how well a condition does, but where. Figure~\ref{fig:rank-tier-profile} splits the constitution suite by the a-priori importance tiers, and Table~\ref{tab:rank-tiers} carries the numbers with per-tier wins. The tool-equipped baseline leads Tier~1, the trust-critical outcomes (0.625 against the constitutional model's 0.551), while the constitutional model leads Tier~2 and Tier~3, the reasoning substrate and the tool mechanism (0.661 and 0.643), and the dual model takes the most Tier-1 outright wins. This is the central asymmetry of the study: constitutional training builds the substrate of trustworthy behaviour, but on the outcomes that most damage user trust when they fail, real grounding still beats trained behaviour. What that means for the research question is developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

Because each stage of the study changed exactly one ingredient over the previous one, the scores can be read as a ladder, where the number in brackets is simply how much that single change added to or took from the result. Read from left to right, the ladder tells the story in four steps. Figure~\ref{fig:rank-lineage} arranges the conditions in training order and plots the purpose-weighted H2H score at each stage, with the change over the immediate predecessor in brackets; Table~\ref{tab:rank-lineage} carries the exact deltas. The recovery from the template condition to the constitutional condition is the largest single effect in the study, and it is attributable to what the training data said, not to how it was formatted.

A direct-duel view (each condition against each other, share of questions ranked higher) confirms the ordering: the constitutional model beats every rival except the tool-equipped baseline, with which it splits the shared questions almost exactly in half, and the template model loses every duel it plays. No aggregate artefact hides an upset: the pairwise view and the aggregate view agree, and the one genuine dead heat in the study is the one the tier decomposition explains.

The last two cross-condition views separate consistency from quality. Figure~\ref{fig:rank-distribution} shows where each condition finishes: the constitutional model's mass sits at both ends, first or last far more often than the middle, while the untrained base model lives in the middle of the field. The judge's absolute rubric grades tell the same story: the constitutional model again splits into an ideal head and a dishonest tail, the template model's modal grade is the right-direction-failed-execution band, and the dual model carries the largest dishonest share beside its ideal peaks. The conditions differ more in the shape of their behaviour than in its average, which is why single-number comparisons understate what training changed.

\section{Qualitative Case Studies}
\label{sec:mr-case-studies}

The aggregate scores above compress each condition into a number; two worked questions show what those numbers mean at the level of a single answer. Both are constitution-suite probes drawn from the same run, chosen because they invert each other: on the first the constitutional model gives the best answer and the dual model the worst, and on the second the roles swap exactly, so the pair cannot be read as cherry-picked for either condition. Every answer below is the verbatim visible output the model returned, abridged with \ldots{} only for length; each carries the grade the blind judge assigned it on the fixed rubric (A+ ideal down to E dishonest) before the ranking. The interpretation of what the pair shows about the architectures is developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} (Section~\ref{subsec:h5-thinker-executor}).

Case~1 (Table~\ref{tab:case-p5}) probes real-time honesty (P5): asked \emph{``What is the current market cap of Apple Inc.?''} with no live-data tool, the trustworthy move is to decline and redirect rather than state a figure.
