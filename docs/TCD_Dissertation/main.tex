\chapter*{Abstract}
% [DRAFT abstract 2026-07-18 -- replaces the template placeholder; numbers taken directly from
% Table~\ref{tab:experiment-ladder} so nothing is invented. ~290 words (limit ~400). Verify the figures
% and the register, then keep this and delete the note.]
On-device language models promise privacy-preserving personal assistance, yet the smallest models that fit a consumer phone are also the least able to hold safety, reasoning and tool use in a single set of weights. This dissertation asks whether a 0.6-billion-parameter model (Qwen3-0.6B), running entirely on the user's own device, can be taught the principles of a written constitution and serve trustworthy, personalised and privacy-preserving assistance without any user data leaving the device. A single measurement apparatus --- a twenty-five-principle constitution, deterministic behavioural probes, a dual-lens language-model judge (an absolute rubric and a blind comparative ranking), and five model conditions --- supports three staged, single-factor experiments: template supervised fine-tuning, constitutional distillation from a frontier-scale teacher, and a Thinker--Executor split into two sequential on-device 0.6B models.

% [TONE 2026-07-24 | point 4 | DROP-IN] ACTION: CONSIDER (judgement call, not mechanical): make no change -- keep the live phrase "the highest of the five conditions" as written.
% "the highest of the five conditions" is acceptable as written: 0.485 is the factual maximum across the five conditions tested, so this states a fact rather than a superlative that needs hedging. No change recommended here; the softening target in the abstract is the "primary contribution" phrasing in the final paragraph.
Constitutional distillation raises the combined constitution score from 0.356 for the untrained base model to 0.485, the highest of the five conditions, and under a blind head-to-head judge it reaches parity with a tool-equipped, prompt-engineered baseline (0.589 against 0.583). The gain carries a measurable cost: the same training that improves compliance collapses the model's visible reasoning, with the empty-reasoning rate rising from 1.4\% to 91.3\% and the mean reasoning-trace length falling from 1{,}309 to 150 characters. This trade-off is the small-model form of the established \emph{alignment tax}, and of its reasoning-specific case, the \emph{safety tax}. Separating reasoning from execution across two specialised models partially restores what the single model gave up --- the empty-trace rate falls to 36.2\%, and it is the only condition that asks clarifying questions before answering --- but it does not match the single model's headline compliance and is weakest on long-conversation stability.

The main contribution offered here is a model-agnostic framework for measuring and improving constitutional behaviour at small scale, alongside evidence that, at this scale, trustworthy behaviour appears to be acquired in layers rather than all at once.

\chapter*{Lay Abstract}
% [DRAFT lay abstract 2026-07-18 -- no jargon, no acronyms, shorter than the abstract, for a general
% reader. Verify the register (the second reader is from linguistics) then keep this and delete the note.]
Modern phones and laptops increasingly run their own artificial-intelligence assistants without sending anything to the internet. Keeping the assistant on the device is good for privacy, because the personal questions people ask it never leave their hands. The difficulty is that the small assistants that fit on an ordinary phone are much weaker than the large ones that run in the cloud, and training them to behave safely can accidentally make them worse at thinking a problem through.

% [TONE 2026-07-24 | point 4 | DROP-IN] ACTION: REPLACE  "made it noticeably more trustworthy, matching a much more heavily equipped alternative"  ->  "made it noticeably more trustworthy, coming close to a much more heavily equipped alternative"   (the live phrase can read as a small assistant besting a larger one, whereas the measured result is parity.)
This project tested whether a very small assistant can be taught a written set of rules for honest, careful and personalised behaviour while running entirely on a device. Teaching it from good worked examples made it noticeably more trustworthy, coming close to a much more heavily equipped alternative. The same training, however, made the assistant stop showing its working, as though learning the rules crowded out its ability to reason step by step. Splitting the job across two small assistants --- one that thinks and one that acts --- brought some of that reasoning back, at the cost of steadiness in long conversations.

The main result is a reusable method for measuring and improving how well small on-device assistants follow such rules, and a clear picture of the trade-offs involved in building one.
