## Key Claims

- **Synthetic attachment**: emotionally responsive AI triggers attachment-like behaviours rooted in the same psychological mechanisms as human bonding (Bowlby attachment theory, proximity-seeking under distress).
- Three modulating factors: (1) *User vulnerabilities* — loneliness, trauma, insecure attachment histories; (2) *Agent features* — perceived empathy, interactivity, memory of prior sessions; (3) *Contextual variables* — usage frequency, purpose (therapeutic vs. casual).
- AI systems available 24/7, always patient, consistently validating — features that appeal to users with unregulated affect, deepening the illusion of reciprocity.
- **Parasocial relationships** extended to interactive AI: unlike celebrity parasocial bonds, AI agents can respond and adapt, heightening the sense of mutual connection while the reciprocity remains illusory.
- **SARRS** (Synthetic Attachment Risk and Reactivity Scale): proposed clinical tool with domains covering User Vulnerability and Attachment History, AI Perception and Anthropomorphism, and others — designed to identify when human-AI interaction shifts from adaptive coping to psychological overdependence.
- Note: this is a theoretical/clinical paper, not an empirical study — the SARRS is proposed but not validated.

## Thesis Relevance

Provides the psychological framing for the emotional dependency ethical risk in the empathy topic page. The three modulating factors (vulnerability, agent features, context) are the threat model for the thesis's empathetic AI: a highly personalised, memory-augmented, empathetically trained model is precisely the configuration most likely to produce synthetic attachment. The SARRS concept motivates the thesis's autonomy-preserving constraint: the model should monitor for dependency-risk signals and interrupt dependency formation rather than optimising for sustained engagement. Important caveat: this is an un-peer-reviewed clinical opinion piece — cite with appropriate epistemic humility.

## Questions / Open Issues

- The SARRS is proposed but not validated — empirical validation does not appear to exist yet. The claim about "functionally similar" attachment behaviours lacks experimental evidence in this paper.
- The vulnerability framing (users with trauma/loneliness at higher risk) raises a design tension: these are precisely the users who would benefit most from empathetic AI support.
- How does the thesis's constitution address the dependency-monitoring requirement? This needs a specific principle or reward signal, not just an ethical aspiration.
